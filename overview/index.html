Are lyrics getting simpler?
4,816 Billboard hits, 1965–2015

Billboard Year-End Hot 100, fifty years

Hit songs doubled in length and repeat more. Their words did not get simpler.

I scored every year-end hit from 1965 to 2015 four ways. The average song went from about 200 words to about 413, and whole songs compress 19% harder, which is what repeated hooks look like to zlib. Vocabulary in the first 100 words rose 7%. The average word was 3.8 characters long in 1965–69 and still 3.8 in 2010–15.

The question

"Simpler" is four measurements, and they disagree

The usual version of this argument rests on a readability score. Readability formulas need sentences, and this dataset has none: the lyrics come lowercased with the punctuation stripped. So I counted four things that need no sentence boundaries: how long a song is, how much it repeats, how varied its vocabulary is, and how long its words are.

Length

Songs got much longer

The yearly mean rises from about 200 words to about 413, roughly 6 words a year, with r = 0.92 against year. It is the cleanest trend in the data. The peak came in the mid-2000s, when 2003's chart averaged 501 words; 2012 dropped back to 369.

Repetition, with a caveat

More repetitive, partly because longer

Run a lyric through zlib and divide the compressed size by the original: text that repeats itself compresses better. The ratio falls from 0.357 to 0.290 (r = −0.89), so later songs repeat more as whole documents.

A longer text also gives the compressor more history to match against, so part of this trend is the length trend measured a second way. The data supports the claim that whole songs repeat more. It says nothing about whether any single line does.

Vocabulary

The vocabulary did not shrink

Here the popular claim fails. Counting only the first 100 words of each song, so that length can't inflate the count, unique words rose from 57.5 to 61.4, about 7% (r = 0.58). That is a small move, in the opposite direction from the dumbing-down story.

Mean word length went from 3.84 to 3.83 characters. With 51 yearly points the slope is statistically detectable (r = −0.46), and it amounts to one hundredth of a character over fifty years. Switch the chart above to the shared scale and the line is flat.

Limitations

What this does not show

The Billboard Year-End Hot 100 is a sales ranking. A change in the chart's genre mix moves every line here even if no songwriter changed how they write, and the analysis does not break results out by genre.

Without punctuation there are no sentences, which rules out the readability formulas this question is usually answered with. Songs under 50 words are dropped, which removes instrumentals and fragments. Each yearly mean rests on 86 to 100 songs, so single years are noisy.

Method

Where the numbers come from

Data
Billboard Year-End Hot 100 lyrics 1965–2015, from the public walkerkq/musiclyrics dataset
Sample
4,816 songs across 51 years, after dropping anything under 50 words
Length
Whitespace tokens per song
Vocabulary
Unique tokens among the first 100, divided by 100
Repetition
zlib (level 6) compressed bytes over raw bytes for the whole lyric
Trend
Least-squares fit of the yearly mean on year (scipy linregress); change compares the 1965–69 and 2010–15 averages
Built with
Python, pandas, SciPy, matplotlib; the chart on this page is hand-written SVG drawn from tables/by_year.csv
