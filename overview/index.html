Are lyrics getting simpler?
4,816 Billboard hits, 1965–2015

Billboard Year-End Hot 100, fifty years

Hit songs doubled in length and repeat more. Their words did not get simpler.

I measured every year-end hit from 1965 to 2015 four ways. The average song went from about 200 words to about 413, and whole songs compress 19% harder (repeated hooks are what zlib sees). Vocabulary in the first 100 words rose 7%, and the average word was 3.8 characters long in 1965–69 and still 3.8 in 2010–15.

The question

"Simpler" covers four separate measurements

The usual version of this argument rests on one statistic, often a readability score. Readability formulas need sentences, and this dataset has none: the lyrics come lowercased with the punctuation stripped.

So I split the question into four things that can be counted without sentence boundaries: how long a song is, how much it repeats, how varied its vocabulary is, and how long its words are. They move in different directions, and that disagreement is the result.

The vocabulary measure counts unique words among each song's first 100 words. Counting unique words across the whole song would mostly re-measure length, since a longer song has more room for new words.

Finding one

Songs got much longer

2.1×

words in the average hit, 2010–15 against 1965–69.

The yearly mean rises from about 200 words to about 413, with a correlation of 0.92 against year, a slope of about 6 words a year. This is the cleanest trend in the data, and the repetition finding below depends on it.

Finding two, with a caveat

More repetitive, partly because longer

Repetition is measured by compression: run the lyric through zlib and divide the compressed size by the original. Text that repeats itself compresses better. The ratio falls from 0.357 to 0.290, with r = −0.89 against year, so later songs repeat more as whole documents.

Length and compressibility are entangled: a longer text gives the compressor more history to match against, so part of this trend is the length finding measured a second way. The data supports the claim that whole songs repeat more, and is silent on whether any given line does.

Finding three

The vocabulary did not shrink

Here the popular claim fails. Unique words among the first 100 rose from 57.5 to 61.4, about 7%, with r = 0.58; a small move, and in the opposite direction from the "dumbing down" story.

Mean word length went from 3.84 to 3.83 characters. The slope is statistically detectable (r = −0.46) and is one hundredth of a character over fifty years, which is no practical change.

Hits got longer and say the same lines more times. The words they use are as varied and as long as they were in 1965.

Limitations

What this does not show

The Billboard Year-End Hot 100 is a sales ranking. It records what sold, and a change in the chart's genre mix moves every line here even if no songwriter changed how they write. The analysis does not break results out by genre, so it cannot say how much of the length trend comes from any one style.

Without punctuation there are no sentences, which rules out the readability formulas this question is usually answered with. Songs under 50 words are dropped, which removes instrumentals and fragments. Every number is a yearly mean of roughly 86 to 100 songs, so single years are noisy.

Method

Where the numbers come from

Data
Billboard Year-End Hot 100 lyrics 1965–2015, from the public walkerkq/musiclyrics dataset
Sample
4,816 songs across 51 years, after dropping anything under 50 words
Length
Whitespace tokens per song
Vocabulary
Unique tokens among the first 100, divided by 100
Repetition
zlib (level 6) compressed bytes over raw bytes for the whole lyric
Trend
Least-squares fit of the yearly mean on year (scipy linregress); change compares the 1965–69 and 2010–15 averages
Built with
Python, pandas, SciPy, matplotlib
