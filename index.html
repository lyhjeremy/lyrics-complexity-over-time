A data story · Billboard Year-End Hot 100, 1965–2015

Are song lyrics getting simpler?

Hit songs doubled in length and repeat more. The words in them are as varied and as long as in 1965.

About 200 words per hit in 1965–69, about 413 in 2010–15.

A common complaint about modern pop is that it repeats the same few words. I tested it against the lyrics of 4,816 year-end Billboard Hot 100 hits from 1965 to 2015, tracking four measures over time: how long songs are, how much they repeat, how varied their vocabulary is, and how long their words are.

Two of the four moved a lot and two barely moved. That split is the answer.

Hits doubled in length

The average charting song went from about 200 words to about 413. It is the strongest trend in the data, rising about 6 words a year.

They repeat more, partly because they are longer

Compress a modern hit with zlib and it shrinks further than a 1960s one, which is the signature of repeated hooks and choruses. A longer text also gives the compressor more to match against, so part of this trend is the length trend measured again.

The words are no simpler

Counting unique words among the first 100 of each song, so that length can't inflate the count, vocabulary rose about 7%. The average word was 3.84 characters in the late 1960s and 3.83 in the 2010s.

So, simpler?

"Simpler" is the wrong word for what changed. Hits are longer and lean harder on repeated hooks, which is what people hear as repetitive. The vocabulary did not shrink. I used the same measures on reality TV in the Love Island lexical analysis and built them into the EPUB reading-level analyzer.

Data and method. Billboard Year-End Hot 100 lyrics, 1965–2015, from the public walkerkq/musiclyrics dataset, 4,816 songs with at least 50 words. The lyrics come lowercased with punctuation stripped, so sentence-based readability scores can't be computed. The chart is a sales ranking, and the analysis does not split results by genre. Reproduce with fetch_data.py and analyze.py in the repo.
